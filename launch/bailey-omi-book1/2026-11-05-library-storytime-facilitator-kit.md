## Run of show — 20 minutes, zero extra equipment required
| Minute | Facilitator action | Child-facing line / question |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 | Show the **actual** printed book cover. Invite quiet observations; do not present mock spreads. | “What do you notice about Bailey and that very tall staircase?” |
| 2–4 | Introduce Bailey and Omi, pointing out how different sisters can help one another. | “Who likes to try new things quickly? Who likes to look first?” |
| 4–11 | Read the **real book** aloud, not a generated replacement script. Pause only at natural moments. | “How might Bailey feel right now?” / “What could help her feel ready?” |
| 11–14 | Reflect without assuming every child has experienced the same emotion. | “Did Bailey need to hurry? Who offered her company?” |
| 14–17 | Lead the **One Small Brave Step** tabletop game below. | “Choose something tiny you could try, with help if you want it.” |
| 17–19 | Invite children to draw or mime an imaginary safe step; opting out is fine. | “What would make trying feel comfortable?” |
| 19–20 | Wrap with gentle invitation to families; show book information if appropriate under library rules. | “A pause can be part of being brave.” |

### One Small Brave Step game (no stairs, no live dog)
The facilitator says three imaginative child-friendly situations: trying a new coloring tool, saying hello to a new library book, or asking for help with a puzzle. After each one, children may choose **look**, **pause**, **try**, or **ask for help**. Each answer is a valid way to approach something new; there are no winners and no simulated dares. If supplies are available, children can draw four small paw prints for these choices. This is **original facilitation copy**, not printed interior text from the book.

### Family take-home text (copy/paste)
**One small step together:** Ask your child, “What felt very big to Bailey?” Then ask, “Who helped her feel safe?” If you have a dog, try a short, comfortable sniffing outing together, with a grown-up supervising and local leash rules followed. Never force a dog onto stairs or into a stressful experience. You don't need a dog to enjoy the story: tell one another about a tiny new thing you might try next week.

### 60-second educator introduction
“Today we're reading *Bailey's Big Climb*, a picture book by S.T. Carroll. Bailey is a little Husky and Omi is her older sister. They are inspired by two real dogs, but this is an illustrated story, not a documentary. We will look at a big staircase, a small puppy, and how encouragement can help without hurrying someone.”
